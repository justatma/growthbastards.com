What if the right move never made your list?

Sales slow down. Someone suggests more ads. Someone else suggests email. Soon the team is debating the same handful of options it debated last quarter.

The MarkOps Atlas puts 537 marketing opportunities on the table. Start with the problem you actually have, see everything else you could try, and find the evidence to choose your next test.

Explore the 537 options
01the evidence

What earns a place in the Atlas?

You could probably name 20 marketing options. Given an afternoon, maybe 50. Getting to 537 took more than collecting suggestions.

The researchers asked the same twelve questions about every option. What would it cost? Where has someone tried it? How would you know whether it helped? Across the Atlas, that meant 6,444 answers to check, including the places where the honest answer was “unknown.”

537opportunities in 26 categories, each with a sourced definition and its own record of what has been checked.
6,444answers checked, twelve questions per opportunity. Where the honest answer was “unknown,” it says so.
388entries added through research. Each has at least two independent primary sources showing the practice exists, and an explanation of how it differs from its nearest neighbor.
132proposed tests across six priority categories. Each names a comparison, an outcome to measure, and something that could mislead the team.

Read this before you pick one

Proof that a practice exists is not proof that it will work for you.

The Atlas tells you what is out there and what is known about it. Whether it pays off for your business is a separate question, and that is what the tests are for.

What a primary source looks like

A primary source is a firsthand account, such as a study by the people who ran it. For one entry on website testing, you can follow the source back to the people who ran 1,379 experiments and reported the wins, the losses and the inconclusive results. Read their study →

02inside the index

One problem can point in very different directions.

Imagine an online store with plenty of visitors and too few orders. Four teams could look at the same numbers and ask four useful questions.

?

Customer research

Ask buyers who chose an alternative what tipped the decision. A missing delivery date might matter more than the price.

?

Positioning and offers

Would a trial or a clearer return promise help someone feel comfortable placing a first order?

?

Website conversion

Walk through checkout on a phone. Find out whether a payment step is losing people who had already decided to buy.

?

Email and customer messaging

Test a cart reminder against sending nothing. Some shoppers would have come back on their own.

From a possible move to a better decision.

Suppose shoppers leave when they see the delivery estimate. The Atlas gives the team several choices, from rewriting the delivery promise to changing where it appears. Here is how the engines are designed to help decide between them.

Find the pattern

Arkwright Engine

Connects customer comments with what people actually did. Did shoppers leave when the estimate appeared, or had checkout already stalled?

Meet Arkwright →
Make it concrete

Creative Intelligence Engine

Uses creative evidence to give the team real alternatives to compare, such as two ways to explain when an order will arrive.

See the CIE →
Weigh the evidence

Bayesian Analytical Engine

Works out how much the evidence changes the decision. “Bayesian” means updating an estimate as new results arrive. A test can strengthen the case for a change, or show why the team needs more evidence.
